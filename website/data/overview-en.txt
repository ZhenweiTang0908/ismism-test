An Overview of the Ismism Framework

1. What the framework is trying to do

The framework uses a four-dimensional matrix to describe different philosophical positions. Instead of treating philosophy as a set of isolated schools, it breaks a position down into four basic questions:

1. What is the background in which the world unfolds?
2. What counts as genuinely existing?
3. How does what exists appear to a subject?
4. What direction should action ultimately take?

The four dimensions are Field, Ontology, Phenomenon, and Purpose. Taken together, they form a rough profile of a philosophical position.

2. The four dimensions

Field

Field does not simply mean “the world.” It means the background system in which things exist and events take place: the relations, rules, structures, and mechanisms that shape a situation. To ask about a field is to ask what larger context makes a particular event possible.

Ontology

Ontology asks not “What do you see?” but “What really exists?” It considers what reality is made of: material objects, institutions, relationships, ideas, subjects, or processes of becoming. Every philosophical position carries an implicit inventory of what it treats as real.

Phenomenon

Phenomenon concerns how being appears to a subject. It is not merely surface appearance, but the way an object is experienced. This dimension therefore has two sides: how something presents itself and how the subject perceives it. It asks whether what we experience is identical with reality itself.

Purpose

Purpose asks what kind of action and meaning the first three dimensions ultimately point toward. It answers the question, “What should we do?” The same analysis of reality can lead to very different actions when the desired end is different.

3. Four basic elements of a position

The framework also proposes four basic elements that can be placed within different dimensions, producing different combinations of philosophical positions:

1. Order
2. Conflict
3. Self-center
4. Nothingness

Order represents a structured, rule-based, repeatable view of the world. It emphasizes stability, sameness, norms, and closure. It can provide certainty, but it can also suppress difference, overlook change, and make a system rigid.

Conflict is not merely an accidental disturbance. It is the tension and contradiction already present within a structure. It shows that an existing order is incomplete, and therefore creates room for change, new ideas, and transformative practice.

Self-center refers to the subject, self-awareness, and the structure of experience organized around “what this means for me.” It emphasizes that our understanding cannot bypass the subject’s position, responsibility, and work of giving meaning.

Nothingness is not simply an empty blank. It is a condition in which negation and openness coexist. It can mean that an old meaning has stopped working, while also opening a space in which new meanings and structures have not yet stabilized.

4. How to understand a result

A result in this framework is not a high or low score. It is a position formed by combining tendencies across several dimensions. For example:

1. A tendency toward Order in Field means that you are more likely to see the world first as a background system that exists before the individual.
2. A tendency toward relations or becoming in Ontology means that you may treat relationships, structures, or processes as more fundamental than static objects.
3. A tendency toward experience in Phenomenon means that you give greater weight to first-person experience and felt meaning, rather than treating experience as a transparent mirror of the outside world.

An assessment result is therefore better understood as a map of how you make sense of the world, not as a single label attached to you.

5. What the current test focuses on

The current test focuses on the first three dimensions:

1. Field: how you understand the background of the world
2. Ontology: how you decide what genuinely exists
3. Phenomenon: how you understand reality appearing to a subject

The test does not directly assess Purpose for now. Its results therefore describe ways of knowing and structures of understanding more than a complete path of action.

6. Principles for interpreting results

1. The result represents a composite philosophical tendency, not membership in one fixed school.
2. It should be understood as a way of making sense of the world, not as an exam score.
3. Each dimension should be explained on its own before considering what the combination means as a whole.
4. An interpretation should remain philosophically careful while staying understandable to a general reader.
